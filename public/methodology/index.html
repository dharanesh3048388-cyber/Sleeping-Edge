Methodology, sleep science & evidence

Last updated: 10 November 2026

SleepingEdge calculators are planning aids. They use the values a visitor enters and clearly stated assumptions. They do not monitor brain activity, measure sleep stages, diagnose a sleep disorder or predict an individual's exact sleep architecture.

Evidence and editorial status

This page summarizes publicly available guidance from the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM), the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NHLBI) and peer-reviewed research. SleepingEdge is evidence-informed but is not currently clinician-reviewed: no named, qualified sleep-medicine professional has formally reviewed or endorsed this website. We will only describe material as expert-reviewed after a genuine review and verification of the reviewer's credentials and permission to be named.

How much sleep is generally recommended?

Sleep needs vary with age and individual circumstances. The CDC's general daily guidance includes:

  • Teenagers aged 13–17: 8–10 hours.
  • Adults aged 18–60: 7 or more hours.
  • Adults aged 61–64: 7–9 hours.
  • Adults aged 65 and older: 7–8 hours.

The AASM and Sleep Research Society joint consensus statement recommends that adults regularly obtain at least seven hours of sleep. These are population-level recommendations, not a personal prescription or a guarantee that one duration will suit everyone.

Sleep cycles and the 90-minute setting

The NHLBI describes sleep as alternating between non-REM and REM phases. A cycle commonly restarts about every 80–100 minutes, and a typical night includes four to six cycles. The pattern changes across the night and varies between people.

SleepingEdge uses 90 minutes as a convenient default for arithmetic comparisons. It is not a universal biological constant. The cycle-length setting lets visitors compare different assumptions; it does not measure or forecast their personal cycle boundaries.

Sleep stages cannot be calculated from clock time

Sleep studies classify stages using physiological measurements, including brain activity and eye movements. A bedtime, alarm time or cycle calculation cannot reveal whether a person is in N1, N2, N3 or REM at a particular moment. Do not reduce sleep opportunity just to target a predicted cycle boundary.

Caffeine estimates

The caffeine calculator uses a first-order decay model and an adjustable half-life assumption. Its default is a modelling input, not a measurement of the visitor's metabolism. Actual caffeine processing varies with dose, individual biology, medicines, pregnancy, liver function, smoking and other factors.

A 2023 systematic review and meta-analysis of 24 studies reported that caffeine, on average, reduced total sleep time by about 45 minutes and sleep efficiency by about 7%, while increasing sleep-onset latency and wake time after sleep onset. The researchers estimated that, in the studies analyzed, a 107 mg coffee serving would need to be consumed about 8.8 hours before bedtime and a 217.5 mg pre-workout serving about 13.2 hours before bedtime to avoid reducing total sleep time. These are study-derived estimates for specific doses, not universal cut-offs or guarantees for an individual.

The calculator's 25 mg remaining-caffeine value is an illustrative model reference only. It is not a clinically established safe threshold. A result below it does not guarantee that caffeine will not affect sleep.

Nap timing

The nap optimizer compares brief and longer opportunities of 20 and 90 minutes. These are simple options in the tool, not guaranteed sleep durations or predictions of sleep stage. Sleep onset, grogginess after waking and the effect of a nap on nighttime sleep vary between people and depend on timing and prior sleep.

What the tools cannot tell you

  • They cannot diagnose insomnia, sleep apnoea or another sleep disorder.
  • They cannot measure sleep stages, sleep quality or circadian rhythm.
  • They cannot determine whether someone is safe to drive or operate machinery.
  • They cannot replace individualized advice from a qualified healthcare professional.

Speak with a qualified healthcare professional if you have persistent sleep difficulties, excessive daytime sleepiness or concerns about your health. Do not use a calculator result to make medication, diagnosis or safety-sensitive decisions.
